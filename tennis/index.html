Tennis Racket Theorem · Physics Explainer

Why does it flip?

1
Step 01 — Anatomy

Every object has three spin axes

Any object flying freely in space has exactly three principal axes of rotation — like three different ways you could spin it. For a tennis racket: like a drill, like a frisbee, or end-over-end.

Any rigid body possesses three mutually perpendicular principal axes of inertia — the eigenvectors of the inertia tensor I. Each axis has an associated scalar moment of inertia I₁ ≤ I₂ ≤ I₃. For a tennis racket: I₁ ≈ handle axis (smallest), I₂ ≈ face normal (intermediate), I₃ ≈ lateral axis (largest).

2
Step 02 — Stability

Two of them are perfectly stable

Spin it around the handle or end-over-end and the rotation is stable. Even if you nudge it off-axis, the wobble bounces back — like a ball in a bowl. The animation shows a real small wobble that stays bounded.

Euler's rotation equations predict rotation about I₁ and I₃ is Lyapunov-stable. Linearizing about ω = ω₀ê₁ gives δ̈ω ~ −λ²δω (λ² > 0) — a harmonic oscillator. The perturbation oscillates at frequency λ but never grows. Both extremal axes sit at energy minima/maxima on the angular-momentum sphere, so perturbations have a restoring force.

3
Step 03 — Instability

The middle axis triggers a chain reaction

Spin it around the face-normal axis and a tiny tilt doesn't bounce back — it amplifies itself. The tilt pushes spin into the handle axis. That pushes spin into the lateral axis. That reverses the face spin. It's a chain reaction, not a gradual drift. The graph shows what it looks like: long flat stretches near ±4 rad/s, then a sudden flip. Small initial nudge → same flips, just slower.

Near I₂, the Euler equations give δ̈ω ~ +λ²δω — a positive feedback (λ² = ω₀²(I₃−I₂)(I₂−I₁)/(I₁I₃) > 0). I₂ is a saddle point of kinetic energy T on the angular-momentum sphere — unlike I₁ (energy max) and I₃ (energy min), it has no restoring force. The ω-vector traces a heteroclinic orbit connecting the two I₂ poles, which is why the face-spin component ωz steps between ±|ω| rather than oscillating smoothly. Flip period: T = 2K(k)/λ.

4
Step 04 — In space

In orbit, it never stops

On Earth, air and your hand dampen the tumbling quickly. In space there's nothing to slow it down. Notice the gold marker — that's the angular momentum vector L, which stays perfectly fixed in space while the racket tumbles wildly around it. Soviet cosmonaut Vladimir Dzhanibekov discovered this with a wing nut aboard Salyut 7 in 1985.

Euler's equations conserve both T = ½ΣIᵢωᵢ² and |L|² = ΣIᵢ²ωᵢ². The ω-vector must lie on the intersection of the energy ellipsoid and the angular-momentum sphere simultaneously. Near I₂ this intersection is a figure-8 heteroclinic orbit connecting two unstable fixed points — the trajectory crosses between hemispheres each flip. The gold indicator shows L fixed in the lab frame; the racket tumbles around it indefinitely without dissipation.

The takeaway

This isn't a quirk of tennis rackets — it happens to any rigid body with three distinct moments of inertia. The intermediate axis is a saddle point of kinetic energy on the angular-momentum sphere: stable when perturbed in one plane, unstable in the other. Asteroids tumble this way. Spacecraft engineers must account for it — satellites need active nutation dampers to bleed energy out of the unstable wobble mode and stay pointing at their targets.

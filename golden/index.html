Two spellings, one number

Key idea

One real number, two coefficients

Multiplying by φ on the real number line means stretching. Multiplying by φ in coefficient space means applying the Fibonacci matrix.

M = 0111 (a, b) → (b, a + b)

Complex-coordinate lens

Read the same pair as a + bi

Replace the basis labels 1 and φ with the familiar complex-plane labels 1 and i. The coordinates do not change, but they can now be read as complex numbers.

The induced complex-coordinate rule x + yi → y + (x + y)i

This is a useful coordinate picture, but it is not ordinary multiplication by a fixed complex constant: it skews the plane instead of rotating and scaling it uniformly.

What the picture means

Coordinates, not extra dimensions

Base-φ numbers themselves lie on the real number line. This 2D grid represents the coefficients of 1 and φ, not two independent spatial dimensions.

The pair (3, 5), for example, is a compact way to record the single real number 3 + 5φ ≈ 11.090. The horizontal movement counts copies of 1; vertical movement counts copies of φ.

Two kinds of multiplication

Complex rotation vs. coefficient transformation

Complex base i − 1

Rotation plus scaling

Complex multiplication naturally acts on a genuine 2D plane. Direction changes and length scales at the same time.

Real base φ

Stretching, seen through coefficients

On the real line, multiplication by φ simply stretches. In coefficient space, the same algebra appears as the skewing map (a, b) → (b, a + b).

Major visual insight

The matrix has two special directions

Most arrows change both length and direction under the Fibonacci matrix. Along an eigen-direction, the arrow stays on the same line.

λ₁ = φ

Expands by φ and keeps its direction.

λ₂ = −1/φ

Contracts by 1/φ and flips direction.

Why Fibonacci numbers appear

Each step adds the last two coefficients

For n ≥ 1, φⁿ = Fₙ₋₁ + Fₙφ. The matrix update is exactly the Fibonacci recurrence.
